        From pricing strategy to simulation model

        1 of 4 shown

        Possible pricing strategies:

        (1) Match competitor, (2) Cost-based, (3) Measure willingness to pay*

        We also need to know our business objective:

        (1) Maximize profit or (2) Maximize market share

        Considerations: price elasticity of demand (sensitivity of demand to price changes)

        We could make predictions of the price by

        (1) Collecting survey data, (2) Participating in a test market or (3) Conducting a real-world experiment

        But we don’t; these methods either have a downside (biased responses and/or too risky/time-consuming) or are too expensive and thus not feasible for us.

        By building a mathematical simulation model and manipulating the model instead of the real world (by taking the price elasticity into account).

        *advanced

        Simulation Modeling

        Simulation (“What if?”) models

        We build and use simulation models so that we can ask “What if?” questions — without manipulating or experimenting with the real world (because that can be difficult, expensive, time-consuming, dangerous, or impossible — remember, we are a student startup that sells cold brew from a campus cart).

        Experiment with the real world

        Change the price on the cart, wait a few weeks, count the bottles. One answer per try, and every wrong try costs real money.

        Experiment with the model

        Change the price in a cell and the outcomes recalculate instantly. Try a hundred prices before lunch; undo anything.

        Put differently, we manipulate the model instead of manipulating the real world.

          Data Table

          Data Table — allows to find optimal prices and quantities

          Aim: Maximize profit by optimized combination of price and quantity

          Discuss first. Then look inside the formulas: the model treats these as fixed.

          • 10 in D5 — the price at which demand drops to zero
          • 123 in D5 — bottles of demand lost for every $1 of price
          • 2500 in E5 — everyone who passes the cart: the whole market
          • 0.75 in F5 — what it costs to brew and bottle one unit

          Beyond the model: competitors’ prices, ingredient costs, tastes.
